ABSTRACT
Basically, customary law is a form of a traditional, commonly accepted rule or practice that has the power of law, and is usually found within a particular ethnic group or location. It doesn't have a formal status but is still recognized in court. The majority of customary laws represent certain community regulations that have been accepted for generations and thus became an inseparable part of the culture of its members. However, one of such traditional practice that has been imbibed into people's culture is the customary law marriage. Customary law represents a traditional, commonly accepted system of rules and practices that possesses the force of law within specific ethnic groups and geographic locations in Nigeria. Although lacking formal codification, customary law enjoys recognition and enforcement by courts of law as an integral component of Nigeria's pluralistic legal system. The majority of customary laws embody community regulations that have been accepted and transmitted across generations, becoming deeply embedded within the cultural fabric and social consciousness of their adherents. Among the various traditional practices governed by customary law, marriage stands out as one of the most significant and universally practiced institutions across Nigerian ethnic communities. This paper provides a comprehensive examination of customary law marriage in Nigeria, exploring its historical foundations, essential requirements, legal implications, and contemporary challenges. The study analyzes the six fundamental prerequisites for valid customary law marriage: betrothal, capacity of the parties, consent (both of the parties and their parents/families), marriage consideration (bride price), solemnization, and consummation. The paper further investigates critical issues including proof and registration of customary marriages, procedures for dissolution, the central role of children in customary unions, and the complex rules governing succession and inheritance under customary law. Particular attention is devoted to examining gender-based discrimination inherent in traditional customary practices, especially regarding women's inheritance rights, widow treatment, and female disinheritance customs. The paper discusses landmark judicial decisions that have struck down discriminatory customs as repugnant to natural justice, equity, and good conscience, including the transformative Ukeje v. Ukeje decision which invalidated female disinheritance under Igbo customary law based on constitutional equality guarantees. The study also explores various specialized forms of customary marriage beyond the standard bride-price marriage, including sororate marriage, woman-to-woman marriage, big and small dowry marriages, and widow inheritance (levirate marriage). Each type is analyzed within its socio-cultural context while considering human rights implications and contemporary legal challenges. This research highlights the ongoing tension between customary law's cultural significance and the imperatives of constitutional rights, particularly regarding gender equality, child protection, and human dignity. The paper concludes by proposing reform pathways that balance cultural preservation with human rights protection, advocating for codification of reformed customary laws, compulsory marriage registration systems, establishment of specialized family courts, and community-based education programs. The analysis demonstrates that while customary law marriage remains deeply rooted in Nigerian culture, its evolution must align with constitutional values and international human rights standards to ensure justice and equity for all citizens, particularly women and children who have historically been disadvantaged under traditional practices.
INTRODUCTION
Nigeria being a multi tribal society, the customs of its people are as varied as the number of ethnic societies. In other words, there is no single customary law for Nigerians. The various ethnic communities have their different customs as they relate to marriage. This paper will explore this topic in a generalized form alluding to the accepted customs within a few communities especially as recognized by the Courts of the land. Marriage under Customary Law is largely polygamous. A polygamous marriage is the union for life of a man with more than one wife. There is no limit to the number of wives a man can marry under customary law. The pluralistic nature of Nigeria's legal system reflects its colonial heritage, where three distinct legal systems operate concurrently: English common law, Islamic law (Sharia), and customary law.1 This legal pluralism creates a complex framework within which marriage is regulated, with customary law marriages constituting the most prevalent form of union, particularly in rural communities where traditional values remain deeply entrenched.2 The Nigerian legal landscape recognizes that customary law, being unwritten and evolving, must continuously adapt to societal changes while maintaining its cultural essence.3 However, it is to be observed that most couples initially get married under customary law and the couples thereafter marry under the Act. This is quite valid provided the marriage is between the same persons. The legal implication of the marriage is that it would then be a monogamous marriage. When a man who is married under the Marriage Act marries another woman under Customary Law, whilst the first marriage subsists, the second marriage is void4. Where a man is married under customary law with one woman subsequently married another woman under the Marriage Act the second marriage is void.5 The interplay between statutory and customary marriages creates significant legal implications regarding the nature and enforceability of marital obligations. The doctrine of conversion, whereby a customary law marriage is transformed into a statutory marriage, has been extensively examined in Nigerian jurisprudence.6 This conversion is irrevocable and permanently alters the legal character of the union from potentially polygamous to strictly monogamous.7 The Supreme Court has consistently held that once a marriage is celebrated under the Marriage 1 Nwauche, E.S. REQUIREMENTS OF A VALID CUSTOMARY LAW MARRIAGE
Generally the following are pre-requisites for a valid customary marriage:
(a) Betrothal
(b) Capacity of the parties
(c) Consent of parties as well as consent of parents/family
(d) Marriage consideration/dowry
(e) Solemnization of the marriage
(f) Consummation of the marriage The above listed perquisites will be considered one after the other. Betrothal Betrothal is simply an agreement to marry. The two parties agree to marry and their parents or families take over. They make discreet investigation of each other's families with a view to deciding if their children should marry into the other family. This process is carried out by intermediaries and if positive, both parties gear up for further ceremonies. Since betrothal is just a mere promise to marry, a breach of the promise to marry is not actionable under customary law9. However, under the Child's Rights Act nobody under the age of 18 can be betrothed to another. The practice of family investigation during betrothal serves multiple sociocultural purposes beyond mere background checks. It functions as a mechanism for establishing inter-familial alliances, assessing genetic compatibility concerns (such as family health history), and determining social standing and reputation.10 In contemporary Nigeria, this investigative process has evolved to include verification of educational qualifications, employment status, and criminal records, reflecting modernization of traditional practices.11 The intermediaries, often respected elders or family friends, play a crucial mediating role in negotiations and dispute resolution throughout the marriage process, acting as traditional witnesses whose testimony may later prove crucial in establishing the validity of the marriage.12 Capacity of the Parties 8 Section 47 Marriage Act; Akinloye v. The parties to a customary law marriage must possess the capacity under that law to marry each other and under this sub topic, we will consider the age of the parties. Age Most systems of customary law in Nigeria do not prescribe any age for the solemnization of customary law marriage. This lacuna in the rule of customary marriage law has to a large extent encouraged a high incidence of child marriage, with all its attendance evils. While in some areas, child betrothal is rampant, marriage does not in fact take place until the parties have attained the age of puberty. Where a girl under sixteen years marries under customary law, the consummation of that marriage does not constitute the sexual offence of having unlawful carnal knowledge of her under the Criminal Code13. This is because Section 6 of the Code define 'unlawful carnal knowledge' to exclude sexual relations between husband and wife. Usually, where a provision of the Code applies only to the husband and wife of a statutory marriage, this is clearly specified, but in the absence of such qualification, it is submitted that 'husband and wife 'includes the parties to a customary law marriage. The issue of child marriage under customary law has generated significant human rights concerns and legislative interventions. Nigeria's ratification of international instruments including the Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC), the African Charter on the Rights and Welfare of the Child, and the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW) imposes obligations to eliminate harmful traditional practices.14 The Child Rights Act 2003 (CRA), which has been adopted by 36 states and the Federal Capital Territory, explicitly prohibits child marriage by setting 18 years as the minimum age for marriage.15 However, implementation remains inconsistent, particularly in northern states where the CRA conflicts with Islamic law provisions that permit marriage at puberty.16 The tension between constitutional guarantees, statutory provisions, and customary/religious practices presents ongoing challenges to uniform child protection standards.17 Consents Two types of consent are relevant to the celebration of customary law marriage and they are: consent of the parties and parental consent

A customary law marriage can be dissolved without pronouncement or intervention. The spouses may decide to break the Union and the usual defences of collusion or condonation under statutory marriage is not available. Adultery (particularly by a wife), loose character, impotence of the husband, sterility of the wife, laziness, ill-treatment and cruelty, leprosy and other harmful disease affecting procreation, witchcraft, addiction to crime and desertion are some of these reasons. The clash between customary succession laws and constitutional equality guarantees has generated transformative jurisprudence reshaping inheritance rights across Nigeria. The principle that "nature abhors discrimination" has been invoked to strike down customs that systematically exclude women and illegitimate children from inheritance.89 Beyond the landmark Ukeje decision, courts have invalidated the Oli-Ekpe custom (Mojekwu), Nrachi custom (Mojekwu v Ejikeme), and various primogeniture practices that subordinate women's inheritance rights.90 However, implementation challenges persist: awareness deficits in rural communities, reluctance of customary court judges to apply constitutional provisions, family pressure on women to waive rights, and lack of enforcement mechanisms undermine theoretical rights.91 The conflict between testamentary freedom and compulsory succession under customary law raises questions about whether individuals can opt out of discriminatory customs through wills, with some courts upholding such wills while others invalidate them for attempting to benefit those customary law excludes.92 Miscellaneous Matters In Respect Of Inheritance Laws As They Affect Women It is clear that many rules of succession under customary law are biased against women or females and the Courts have in recent past risen to the defence of women by declaring some of these customs as repugnant to natural justice, equity and good conscience. Other types of customary law marriage Beside the strict bride-price marriage, which is the commonest type, and the one most commonly contracted, there are other relationships which are regarded as marriage in customary law. While some of them possess the characteristics of a bride price marriage, other display different and unique features.
(A) SORORATE MARRIAGE
In this type of relationship, on the death of a wife, the widower may be presented with a substitute by her family without the fresh marriage procedure. In such cases, the first marriage is regarded as continuing, with the new wife 99 Per Clara Bata Ogunbiyi, JSC taking the place of the deceased relative. It has been established that sororate marriage is not part of Ibo customary law. No evidence exists today of its survival anywhere in the country.
(B) WOMAN TO WOMAN MARRIAGE
Under some customary laws in Nigeria, certain marriages are contracted which may superficially be described as the union of two women. On the surface, such arrangement may be said to contravene the basic precept of marriage as a union between a man and a woman. However, there is much in these cases than meets the eye. The true position in each case is that there is at the background, a man in whose name or behalf the marriage is contracted. Some illustrations may serve to bring out the point. Sometimes, a barren married woman, as a means of securing her position in the family, provides her husband with funds for the bride-price in respect of a new wife who is expected to bear children in her place. In that case100, the marriage is in fact contracted in the name of the husband and there is no question of one woman being married to another. In some parts of Nigeria, an unmarried but prosperous woman who desires to have a family of her own may, if she cannot bear children, `marry` another woman to do so on her behalf. She attains this objective by providing the bride-price for a new wife who while living with her bears children. Usually, internal family arrangements are made whereby the new wife bears children by specially chosen male member of the family or by a paramour101. The marriage is in effect contracted in the name of a male member of the family of the financier thereof, and may be regarded as the type of `ghost marriage`.
(C) BIG DOWRY AND SMALL DOWRY MARRIAGE
In the riverine areas of ijaw, for instance, Okrika, there are two type of marriage. The incident of each depends on the quantum of the bride price paid. Where the iya or 'big dowry' marriage is contracted, the woman becomes part of her husband's family and her children belong to that family also. But in the Igwa, or 'small dowry' marriage, the woman remains part of her original family and her children become member of their mother`s family and inherit from it. But the distinction between this system of marriage is rapidly breaking down. This has been due to social and legislative changes, of which the Eastern Region Limitation of Dowry Law, 1956 is the most significant. The law, it will be recalled, limited bride price in respect of customary law marriage to not more than sixty naira. The effect is to blur the distinction between the bride-price payable in respect of iya and igwa marriages. Other factors are the declining economic importance of the house system and the acceptance of European and Christian ideas of monogamy.

CONCLUSION
As seen above, marriage has a prestigious and foremost place in the Nigerian culture like in other African cultures. Customarily, so much importance is attached to the institution of marriage and it is a normal condition for both men and women. It is also evident from above that despite the variances present in the different cultures, there are similar basic requirements that validate the union between a man and a woman, however civilisation has brought about a change in perception regarding certain principles in these cultures and norms as some of the practices and beliefs has been declared repugnant to natural justice, equity and good conscience. Certain laws has also been put in place to mitigate the complexity caused by some of the customary practices. It is however hoped that the various ethnic groups will operate in line with current trend and do away with obsolete practices.
